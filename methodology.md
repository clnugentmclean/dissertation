There are various ways to use digital tools and methods to analyze a corpus of plain text documents. This project primarily uses word embedding models and AntConc. Word embedding models are “a set of techniques from machine learning and natural language processing (NLP) that model textual data numerically, using mathematical relationships like vectors to map semantic relationships between words in a corpus.”  To put it another way, word embedding models are able to analyze the ways in which words appear in similar contexts in a corpus, as well as being able to process very large amounts of textual data. These models require a large amount of data from which the algorithm “learns” the contexts between words.  For example, take the names of days of the week like “Monday” and “Tuesday.” Models trained on most corpora will likely find that the closest words to these terms (that is, the words that share similar linguistic contexts) are other days of the week, even though these words do not necessarily appear near each other in the corpus.

According to Benjamin Schmidt, word embedding models both “try to reflect similarities in usage between words to distance in space” and “try to reflect similar relationships between words with similar paths in space.”  In other words, word embedding models assign numerical values to words in a text based on their relation to other words. These numerical representations are known as ‘word vectors’ and they help describe the distance between words and how they are used.  Word embedding models seek to map semantic proximity, which considers the meaning of the words itself within the specific context of the training corpus. Word vectors measure semantic proximity with cosine similarities. Cosine similarities are numerical representations of the ‘closeness’ between words in vector space. Cosine similarity is more concerned with the orientation of the two points in space, rather than their exact distance from one another.  Essentially, vectors from the same corpus are two lines that share an origin point. Connecting those lines forms a triangle, and calculating the distance between the vectors can be done using the cosine of that line.  (Picture both lines starting from the corner of an x/y plot and then connecting both points with a third line, which would form a triangle. Calculating the length of that connective line is how to determine the cosine similarity.) The larger the cosine number, the closer those two vectors are. 

The ability to search for keywords is not unique to word embedding models. Because word embedding models attempt to consider the context of the word, it is possible to see word associations that would not be seen by analyzing words that appear near each other. For this project, the digital tool AntConc serves as the bridge connecting word embedding models and primary close reading. AntConc, developed by Lawrence Anthony, is an open-source corpus analysis toolkit for concordancing, collocates, and other text analysis.  Word embedding models, as mentioned above, can provide a large-scale almost birds-eye view of an entire textual corpus, identifying trends, patterns, and frequency in a way that is almost impossible for a single human historian to acquire. However, these models can be criticized for divorcing the words from their specific context, i.e. the specific article, or even sentence, they appear in. After uploading a large text corpus, AntConc can search specific terms; unlike in a word embedding model, AntConc displays every instance of the search query within the article it appears. Concordances show a term’s occurrences with its surrounding context, or to put it another way, they identify the words that surround a query term every time it appears in a text; the concordance tool in AntConc is called ‘Key Words in Context’ (KWIC). For this project, the KWIC tool shows the surrounding context for every instance of each keyword. This keyword search tool specifically is central to bridging the divide between primary close reading and the word embedding models. For example, AntConc can provide every article the word mussulman is mentioned in, even once, and then close reading can be done on all the articles. This is a more sophisticated form of keyword searching compared to digital archives and online databases. 

The ‘Collocates’ tool generates a list of words that frequently occur in the context of the search term. To put another way, this tool allows for the investigation of non-sequential patterns in language. The tool provides the frequency of words appearing to the left or the right of the search query; this value measures how ‘related’ the search term and the collocate are. Collocates are like concordances, with the addition of statistical analysis. Collocates give statistical insights into the relationships seen in the concordances. AntConc also justifies the use of word embedding models themselves, because it demonstrates how often the query terms appear close together (i.e. within 5-10 words). For all my case studies, words for Muslims and words like fanatical and rebellious only appear near each other a handful of times, proving keyword searching alone does not adequately answer my research question about anti-Muslim rhetoric. 

Focusing on *The Times* of London is a question of reach, and what is feasible within the confines of a single dissertation. The newspaper has also been fully digitized by Gale, available through [*The Times* Digital Archive](https://www.gale.com/product-catalog/266619), and of course digitization is a necessary requirement for computational analysis. Scope notwithstanding, *The Times* held an influential place within British culture for the entirety of the period of study, making it a useful proxy for studying public opinion.  It was one of few national newspapers in 1857 and continued to maintain relevance throughout the period of study. Notably, its content tended to be reproduced in smaller papers across Britain giving it a very wide readership, especially in the nineteenth century.  There are also initial studies using word embedding models with a corpus of *The Times*, indicating its utility as a dataset for analysis.  Additionally, focusing on a single paper allows me to create a through-line between all of the case studies here. 

This project is informed by the theory that semantic change is a useful proxy for understanding broader historical processes , and will consider the language within newspapers as one means of assessing general perception. Importantly, my primary evidence does not come exclusively from *The Times*, as I am accompanying the digital analysis with traditional close reading of other sources such as published contemporary historical accounts, articles published in literary magazines, pamphlets, and public lectures. Because I am interested in the effect of these events on public opinion and discourse, I do not use any private correspondence or military memos. 
All my corpora were created using the OCR (Optical Character Recognition) version of each issue of *The Times* of London.  OCR refers to the identification of printed characters using computer software, which is essentially the process of turning a PDF into plain text. Building a corpus for a humanities text analysis project is extremely labor intensive.  I built four corpora in total, which took three years of manual data identification, collection, and cleaning. For each case study, I created a corpus that spans three years, with the year of the event under consideration as the median year; i.e. the corpus for the 1898 Battle of Omdurman spanned from 1897 to 1899. The 1857 Rebellion is a unique case because the event itself lasted from May 1857 to November 1858. Due to this, the corpus for the Rebellion spans 1856 to 1860 because the corpus for each case study includes one year before and after the event. My plain text files are organized by each issue of *The Times*. The size of the corpora for each case study is immense. For the first chapter, the corpus of *The Times* from 1856 to 1859 contained a bit more than 80 million words (or, more specifically, 80,062,149 words). For the second chapter, the corpus from 1897 to 1899 contained almost 86 million words (85,955,610 words to be exact), and the third chapter’s corpus contained almost 137 million words (136,705,720 words). 

This project uses the full issues of each newspaper, as opposed to only selecting articles that are explicitly discussing the case study event. The entirety of the newspaper, including advertisements, creates a broader and more complete picture of the discourse within *The Times*. It is important to note here that the decision to include advertisements is a significant one from the perspective of a digital project. Due to their frequency, advertisements have the potential to overwhelm word embedding models and dominate much of the results. For example, if I was concerned with the most common word vectors in general across *The Times*, advertisements could skew the results by making certain turns of phrase seem more popular when they were simply ad copy. The tendency of advertisements to be repeated in multiple issues can cause results that seem to show a dominance of certain words. However, the issue of advertisements is more salient when trying to make general claims about the shape of The Times at large, and is not as significant when asking specific questions. Because this project is looking more specifically at words related to Islam, I have decided not to remove advertisements to avoid making editorial changes to my corpora. 

It is essential to consider what changes, if any, should be made to a corpus, as these changes will affect the results. This project uses standard pre-processing conventions to clean the corpora prior to model training. These conventions include removing punctuation, numbers, and non-character tokens, and making every token lower case. While regularizing the spellings of certain words can make the results easier to interpret, it is still a manipulation that needs to be highlighted, and it is good practice to analyze results from an unmanipulated corpus as well. Manipulations can include the decision to remove advertisements, fix OCR errors, regularize the spellings of words, or even identify certain articles to omit.

One important step in my methodology was to create a shortlist of terms that would be most useful to better answer my research question. Creating queries around the word Muslim for the earlier chapters was not useful, for example, as this is too modern a term; instead, words like *mahomedan*, *mussulman*, and even *moslem* were more relevant to the time. It is also important to mention that spellings of these words were not as standardized at the time; for example, during this period *muhammadan, mahommedan, mohomedan, mahometan*, and *mohammedan* were all valid spellings of the term for Muslims in the mid-nineteenth century. The presence of *mohamedanism* and *mahomedans* (words used almost exclusively by non-Muslims) represent the positionality of the contributors of *The Times*. By defining the religion through the figure of Mohammed, these terms create a false analogy between the role of Jesus Christ in Christianity and Mohammed in Islam. Unlike in Christianity, where followers see Jesus Christ himself as a divine figure, Mohammed in Islam is a human prophet who shares the word of God. Therefore, the presence of these terms alone highlights the othering of Muslims within The Times. It was essential to create a different shortlist of query terms for each corpus, based on the popularity of terms during each individual period.

Different variations of words for Muslims were not the only aspect of my data that required consideration. Fixing OCR (Optical Character Recognition) errors, often called OCR noise, is another form of corpus manipulation especially relevant for this project. For example, the average accuracy of OCR for the 1856-1859 corpus was 81%. The accuracy of the OCR improves in relation to how modern the corpus is, in part because printing processes were much clearer and more standardized in 1914 than in 1856. Due to the accuracy range of 81%–88%, OCR noise does impact model performance. The kind of algorithms that word2vec uses, specifically skip-gram and Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW), tend to perform better in terms of resiliency to OCR noise when compared against other popular word embedding algorithms.  However, OCR noise remains a major challenge in text analysis of historical documents. 

There are multiple variations of words for Muslims (*mussulman* and *moslem* for example), but there were also many OCR (mis)spellings of these words (*mussulman* and *msuslman* for example). Word embedding models treat each misspelling as its own word (or vector). That meant there was a multitude of vectors created for every spelling instance for words for Muslims in the corpus, reducing the opportunities for the model to learn about the language of the corpus. To rectify this issue, I trained multiple models on the unmanipulated corpus first and then used these models to identify the top (mis)spellings of words for Muslims. This allowed me to return to the corpus itself and regularize all the spellings for these words using find/replace in BBEdit. For example, the word *mahomedan* had the following variations in the unmanipulated 1856–1859 corpus:*mlahomedan, miahomedan, mahomnedan, mahometan*, and *mahommedan*.  After using the trained models to identify all these instances, I then returned to the original corpus and regularized all these spellings to *mahomedan*. This meant the manipulated corpus considered all the varying instances as part of the same word vector, so it had a greater opportunity to understand the language. 

Setting the parameters for word vector training has significant results on the model, but the models can vary even with the same parameters. Word2vec models are nondeterministic, meaning that the models will differ even when trained on the same corpus with the same parameters. It is best practice to train multiple models with the same parameters to ensure that none of the resultant models are giving dramatically different results. 

The window size parameter determines the size of the window that is sliding along the text when the model is trained. For example, a window size of 5 means that the window will look at the five words before the target word, the target word, and the five words after. The words before and after form the context of the target word. Every single word in the corpus is treated as the target word in the process of training, which is part of why training a word embedding model takes significant processing power and time. Some claim a larger window size leads to embeddings where cosine similarity reveals the semantic relations of words, as opposed to smaller window sizes which can produce “interchangeable” words.  In other words, larger windows tend to contain more semantic information, while shorter windows tend to contain more syntactical information. Choosing an optimal window size for word embedding models is ultimately at the discretion of the researcher, although some suggest that the window should not be too small (i.e. <5 words).  
    
To demonstrate what the window size parameters does, I trained a model on my Rushdie Affair corpus (December 1, 1988-March 31, 1989) with a window size of 2 and a window size of 11. In **Figure 1**, I queried the closest words to fast for both the models because common adjectives can be useful in visualizing the difference between semantics and syntax.

**Figure 1** included all words with at least 0.60 cosine similarity to fast for the model trained with a window size of 11. The smaller the window size, the less contextual information the model has to build its embedding, thus smaller window sizes tend to also feature lower cosine similarities. This can be seen in **Figure 1**, where there were only two terms that had at least 0.60 cosine similarity to fast. The presence of mouthwatering highlights the issues that come with using a too small window size, notably the higher chance of erroneous results.  

Because smaller windows tend to preserve syntactical information, they are more likely to relate terms by their structural similarities, rather than their meanings. This can be seen in **Figure 1** where all the terms with at least 0.55 cosine similarity appear in the same syntax as fast.  The words rapidly, slow, slowly, and fastest could all be “interchanged” syntactically with fast without changing the structure of the original sentence. Compared to the window size of 2, the semantic relationships are much clearer in the window 11 model. Of the terms that had at least 0.65 cosine similarity (moving, paced, rapidly, dynamic, exciting, highly, expanding, stimulating, busy, demanding, challenging, rewarding), only rapidly shares that syntactical relationship seen in the window size of 2. The difference between semantic and syntactical relationships can be seen through the presence of paced (with a cosine of 0.71) in the window 11 model and its much lower cosine (0.52) in the window 2 model. If you were to exchange fast for paced within a sentence, it likely would not make much sense demonstrating that the relationship between those terms is not based on syntax. The relationship between the two terms is based on the meaning of the words and how those meanings relate to each other (i.e. in the idea of a “fast pace”). That being said, both models still identify some of the same words as closest to fast, like rapidly and growing; this demonstrates how even with varying window size parameters both models are creating embeddings that make sense to human readers.

The vector size parameter controls the dimensionality of the model. A higher number of dimensions can make the model more precise, while at the same time increasing the possibility of random errors.   To demonstrate the difference between vector size, I trained two models, with 100 vectors and 500 vectors, on my second chapter’s full corpus (1897–1899).  The results between the 500 vector models and the models with 100 vectors were similar, though the 500-vector model had much lower cosine similarity across the board. Both training and analysis of the 500-vector model took much more time and processing power. Figure 2 below shows the top twenty closest words to cold for a 500-vector model and a 100-vector model. The highest cosine similarities in the 500-vector model is 0.64, which is noticeably lower than the highest in the 100-vector model of 0.82. This is likely because training word embedding models with fewer dimensions can result in more overlapping vector angles, which causes higher average similarity scores. The top twenty results for both models share 13 words (a majority). The words that appear only in the 500-vector model are: colder, cool, frost, showers, foggy, rains, and wvinds. The words that appear only in the 100-vector model are: wveather, unseasonable, chilly, moist, soft, wintry, and cooler. Since these models do not have dramatically different results, it made sense to use the 100-vector parameter. These reasons, combined with the heavier processing time and power required for the 500-vector, is why I used 100-vector models for this project.

The model algorithm determines word embeddings, in part, by looking at which other words tend to appear next to the target word. If all example pairs are positive, i.e. neighbors, this can create a bad model that claims 100% accuracy.  Embedding models need negative samples, or samples of words that are not neighbors, as part of the training process to produce a good model. Best practices suggest that between 5 and 20 is a good range for negative samples when training a word embedding model.  While adjusting the parameter can affect the model, it is not nearly as visible as adjusting the window size and number of vectors. I trained a model with 5 negative samples and 15 negative samples, and the effect is not immediately noticeable in the results. 
For all my case studies, I trained a variety of models using both identical and different parameters for the full corpus. In total, I trained five models with identical parameters  on the full corpus for each chapter. I trained each corpus five times with the exact same parameters, to ensure that the results were not drastically different. Because word embedding models are nondeterministic, it is important to compare multiple models trained on the same parameters to ensure there are not dramatic changes. Results that consistently appear across models are much more significant than the specific placement of words on lists for an individual model. In each chapter, I go into more detail on the parameters used to train the corpus.

For example, **Figure 3** above shows the top twenty closest words to fanatical for all five of the models for the 1856–1859 corpus. While there is some variation across all the models (most noticeably in the specific placement on the lists) these models are all performing very similarly. Word embedding models must be evaluated to better understand the results. This project uses the word2vec algorithm for word embedding. Evaluating word embedding models should be tailored to suit the goals of the specific research project. A principle for evaluating a model looks at the quality of the model’s placement of words in vector space, essentially asking if the model shows results that make sense to a human reader. One of the standard methods for evaluating word embedding models involves generating word pairs that should be related in some way (examples of word pairs include: girl-boy, day-night, and children-parents) and calculating the cosine similarities for those pairs.  It is essential to test the model to make sure it is working correctly before beginning to use it for specific research queries. 

I evaluated the models using word pairs that are likely to have high cosine similarity. To put this another way, word pairs are words that humans would expect to have high cosine similarities due to our understanding of language. These do not necessarily have to be synonyms (example dangerous-perilous), in fact, sometimes antonyms work even better in generating close relations (example hot-cold). For all of the models, I evaluated them using 170 different word-pairs. Selecting what word pairs to use to evaluate the models is a matter of judgment and knowing the corpus.  I am particularly interested in the way the model understands texts related to military affairs, so my list of word pairs includes a variety related to the military; battle-war, battle-fight, officers-soldiers, officers-generals, troops-army, troops-cavalry, war-campaign, war-peace, and war-mutiny are all word pairs used to evaluate all of the models. 

One of the biggest difficulties in evaluating word embedding models is that there is still no agreed upon process, or best practices.   I evaluated all of the models on a changing list of word-pairs that I adjusted as I got a better understanding of the language being reflected in the corpus, before re-evaluating all the models on the finalized list of word-pairs.  For the majority of these 170 word-pairs, the models had cosine similarities between 0.6 and 0.8, a good sign that the model is working effectively. The process of evaluating and testing models described above was used for the corpora of each chapter. Each chapter briefly describes the parameters and evaluation of the specific models, but the detail of the methodology is found in this introduction.

My methodology explores three [case studies](https://clnugentmclean.github.io/dissertation/research/casestudies/) from British history that represent significant engagement with ideas about Islam, using digital text analysis of periodicals surrounding each of these cases. I demonstrate that the anti-Muslim rhetoric that became so common in the British press—including the idea that Islam is incompatible with British culture and values due to its supposed inherent fanatic nature—were already well-established in British discourse long before 1989. Although language in general shifts over time, I argue that certain contours of language surrounding Muslims within the corpus of *The Times* are consistent over this half century. Importantly, I am not claiming that the discourse and rhetoric surrounding Islam in *The Times* has not changed at all over this period. Rather, my use of word embedding models demonstrates certain essential semantic relationships that have stayed consistent, including the association of fanaticism with Muslims, while the close reading of the same sources reveals a shift in intensity, or fear, dependent on the presence (or not) of British women and what that meant for British dominance in the colonies. Oftentimes, Britons at home paid more attention to colonial events that risked women and children. I argue that there is consistency in anti-Muslim rhetoric across the period, but the degree of perceived outrage or danger determined degree of vitriol and fear in the discourse.
