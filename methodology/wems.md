This project uses word embedding models as the primary methodology. According to Benjamin Schmidt, word embedding models both “try to reflect similarities in usage between words to distance in space” and “try to reflect similar relationships between words with similar paths in space.”  In other words, word embedding models assign numerical values to words in a text based on their relation to other words. These numerical representations are known as ‘word vectors’ and they help describe the distance between words and how they are used.  Word embedding models seek to map semantic proximity, which considers the meaning of the words itself within the specific context of the training corpus. Word vectors measure semantic proximity with cosine similarities. Cosine similarities are numerical representations of the ‘closeness’ between words in vector space. Cosine similarity is more concerned with the orientation of the two points in space, rather than their exact distance from one another.  Essentially, vectors from the same corpus are two lines that share an origin point. Connecting those lines forms a triangle, and calculating the distance between the vectors can be done using the cosine of that line.  (Picture both lines starting from the corner of an x/y plot and then connecting both points with a third line, which would form a triangle. Calculating the length of that connective line is how to determine the cosine similarity.) The larger the cosine number, the closer those two vectors are. 

Setting the parameters for word vector training has significant results on the model, but the models can vary even with the same parameters. Word2vec models are nondeterministic, meaning that the models will differ even when trained on the same corpus with the same parameters. It is best practice to train multiple models with the same parameters to ensure that none of the resultant models are giving dramatically different results. 

The window size parameter determines the size of the window that is sliding along the text when the model is trained. For example, a window size of 5 means that the window will look at the five words before the target word, the target word, and the five words after. The words before and after form the context of the target word. Every single word in the corpus is treated as the target word in the process of training, which is part of why training a word embedding model takes significant processing power and time. Some claim a larger window size leads to embeddings where cosine similarity reveals the semantic relations of words, as opposed to smaller window sizes which can produce “interchangeable” words.  In other words, larger windows tend to contain more semantic information, while shorter windows tend to contain more syntactical information. Choosing an optimal window size for word embedding models is ultimately at the discretion of the researcher, although some suggest that the window should not be too small (i.e. <5 words).  
    
To demonstrate what the window size parameters does, I trained a model on my Rushdie Affair corpus (December 1, 1988-March 31, 1989) with a window size of 2 and a window size of 11. In **Figure 1**, I queried the closest words to fast for both the models because common adjectives can be useful in visualizing the difference between semantics and syntax.

**Figure 1** included all words with at least 0.60 cosine similarity to fast for the model trained with a window size of 11. The smaller the window size, the less contextual information the model has to build its embedding, thus smaller window sizes tend to also feature lower cosine similarities. This can be seen in **Figure 1**, where there were only two terms that had at least 0.60 cosine similarity to fast. The presence of mouthwatering highlights the issues that come with using a too small window size, notably the higher chance of erroneous results.  

Because smaller windows tend to preserve syntactical information, they are more likely to relate terms by their structural similarities, rather than their meanings. This can be seen in **Figure 1** where all the terms with at least 0.55 cosine similarity appear in the same syntax as fast.  The words rapidly, slow, slowly, and fastest could all be “interchanged” syntactically with fast without changing the structure of the original sentence. Compared to the window size of 2, the semantic relationships are much clearer in the window 11 model. Of the terms that had at least 0.65 cosine similarity (moving, paced, rapidly, dynamic, exciting, highly, expanding, stimulating, busy, demanding, challenging, rewarding), only rapidly shares that syntactical relationship seen in the window size of 2. The difference between semantic and syntactical relationships can be seen through the presence of paced (with a cosine of 0.71) in the window 11 model and its much lower cosine (0.52) in the window 2 model. If you were to exchange fast for paced within a sentence, it likely would not make much sense demonstrating that the relationship between those terms is not based on syntax. The relationship between the two terms is based on the meaning of the words and how those meanings relate to each other (i.e. in the idea of a “fast pace”). That being said, both models still identify some of the same words as closest to fast, like rapidly and growing; this demonstrates how even with varying window size parameters both models are creating embeddings that make sense to human readers.

The vector size parameter controls the dimensionality of the model. A higher number of dimensions can make the model more precise, while at the same time increasing the possibility of random errors.   To demonstrate the difference between vector size, I trained two models, with 100 vectors and 500 vectors, on my second chapter’s full corpus (1897–1899).  The results between the 500 vector models and the models with 100 vectors were similar, though the 500-vector model had much lower cosine similarity across the board. Both training and analysis of the 500-vector model took much more time and processing power. **Figure 2** below shows the top twenty closest words to cold for a 500-vector model and a 100-vector model. The highest cosine similarities in the 500-vector model is 0.64, which is noticeably lower than the highest in the 100-vector model of 0.82. This is likely because training word embedding models with fewer dimensions can result in more overlapping vector angles, which causes higher average similarity scores. The top twenty results for both models share 13 words (a majority). The words that appear only in the 500-vector model are: colder, cool, frost, showers, foggy, rains, and wvinds. The words that appear only in the 100-vector model are: wveather, unseasonable, chilly, moist, soft, wintry, and cooler. Since these models do not have dramatically different results, it made sense to use the 100-vector parameter. These reasons, combined with the heavier processing time and power required for the 500-vector, is why I used 100-vector models for this project.

The model algorithm determines word embeddings, in part, by looking at which other words tend to appear next to the target word. If all example pairs are positive, i.e. neighbors, this can create a bad model that claims 100% accuracy.  Embedding models need negative samples, or samples of words that are not neighbors, as part of the training process to produce a good model. Best practices suggest that between 5 and 20 is a good range for negative samples when training a word embedding model.  While adjusting the parameter can affect the model, it is not nearly as visible as adjusting the window size and number of vectors. I trained a model with 5 negative samples and 15 negative samples, and the effect is not immediately noticeable in the results. 

For all my case studies, I trained a variety of models using both identical and different parameters for the full corpus. In total, I trained five models with identical parameters  on the full corpus for each chapter. I trained each corpus five times with the exact same parameters, to ensure that the results were not drastically different. Because word embedding models are nondeterministic, it is important to compare multiple models trained on the same parameters to ensure there are not dramatic changes. Results that consistently appear across models are much more significant than the specific placement of words on lists for an individual model. In each chapter, I go into more detail on the parameters used to train the corpus.

Word embedding models must be evaluated to better understand the results. This project uses the word2vec algorithm for word embedding. Evaluating word embedding models should be tailored to suit the goals of the specific research project. A principle for evaluating a model looks at the quality of the model’s placement of words in vector space, essentially asking if the model shows results that make sense to a human reader. One of the standard methods for evaluating word embedding models involves generating word pairs that should be related in some way (examples of word pairs include: girl-boy, day-night, and children-parents) and calculating the cosine similarities for those pairs.  It is essential to test the model to make sure it is working correctly before beginning to use it for specific research queries. 

I evaluated the models using word pairs that are likely to have high cosine similarity. To put this another way, word pairs are words that humans would expect to have high cosine similarities due to our understanding of language. These do not necessarily have to be synonyms (example dangerous-perilous), in fact, sometimes antonyms work even better in generating close relations (example hot-cold). For all of the models, I evaluated them using 170 different word-pairs. Selecting what word pairs to use to evaluate the models is a matter of judgment and knowing the corpus.  I am particularly interested in the way the model understands texts related to military affairs, so my list of word pairs includes a variety related to the military; battle-war, battle-fight, officers-soldiers, officers-generals, troops-army, troops-cavalry, war-campaign, war-peace, and war-mutiny are all word pairs used to evaluate all of the models. 

One of the biggest difficulties in evaluating word embedding models is that there is still no agreed upon process, or best practices.   I evaluated all of the models on a changing list of word-pairs that I adjusted as I got a better understanding of the language being reflected in the corpus, before re-evaluating all the models on the finalized list of word-pairs.  For the majority of these 170 word-pairs, the models had cosine similarities between 0.6 and 0.8, a good sign that the model is working effectively. The process of evaluating and testing models described above was used for the corpora of each chapter. Each chapter briefly describes the parameters and evaluation of the specific models, but the detail of the methodology is found in this introduction.
