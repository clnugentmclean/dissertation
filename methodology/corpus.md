Focusing on *The Times* of London is a question of reach, and what is feasible within the confines of a single dissertation. The newspaper has also been fully digitized by Gale, available through [*The Times* Digital Archive](https://www.gale.com/product-catalog/266619), and of course digitization is a necessary requirement for computational analysis. Scope notwithstanding, *The Times* held an influential place within British culture for the entirety of the period of study, making it a useful proxy for studying public opinion.  It was one of few national newspapers in 1857 and continued to maintain relevance throughout the period of study. Notably, its content tended to be reproduced in smaller papers across Britain giving it a very wide readership, especially in the nineteenth century.  There are also initial studies using word embedding models with a corpus of *The Times*, indicating its utility as a dataset for analysis.  Additionally, focusing on a single paper allows me to create a through-line between all of the case studies here. 

This project is informed by the theory that semantic change is a useful proxy for understanding broader historical processes , and will consider the language within newspapers as one means of assessing general perception. Importantly, my primary evidence does not come exclusively from *The Times*, as I am accompanying the digital analysis with traditional close reading of other sources such as published contemporary historical accounts, articles published in literary magazines, pamphlets, and public lectures. Because I am interested in the effect of these events on public opinion and discourse, I do not use any private correspondence or military memos. 

All my corpora were created using the OCR (Optical Character Recognition) version of each issue of *The Times* of London.  OCR refers to the identification of printed characters using computer software, which is essentially the process of turning a PDF into plain text. Building a corpus for a humanities text analysis project is extremely labor intensive.  I built four corpora in total, which took three years of manual data identification, collection, and cleaning. For each case study, I created a corpus that spans three years, with the year of the event under consideration as the median year; i.e. the corpus for the 1898 Battle of Omdurman spanned from 1897 to 1899. The 1857 Rebellion is a unique case because the event itself lasted from May 1857 to November 1858. Due to this, the corpus for the Rebellion spans 1856 to 1860 because the corpus for each case study includes one year before and after the event. My plain text files are organized by each issue of *The Times*. The size of the corpora for each case study is immense. For the first chapter, the corpus of *The Times* from 1856 to 1859 contained a bit more than 80 million words (or, more specifically, 80,062,149 words). For the second chapter, the corpus from 1897 to 1899 contained almost 86 million words (85,955,610 words to be exact), and the third chapter’s corpus contained almost 137 million words (136,705,720 words). 

This project uses the full issues of each newspaper, as opposed to only selecting articles that are explicitly discussing the case study event. The entirety of the newspaper, including advertisements, creates a broader and more complete picture of the discourse within *The Times*. It is important to note here that the decision to include advertisements is a significant one from the perspective of a digital project. Due to their frequency, advertisements have the potential to overwhelm word embedding models and dominate much of the results. For example, if I was concerned with the most common word vectors in general across *The Times*, advertisements could skew the results by making certain turns of phrase seem more popular when they were simply ad copy. The tendency of advertisements to be repeated in multiple issues can cause results that seem to show a dominance of certain words. However, the issue of advertisements is more salient when trying to make general claims about the shape of The Times at large, and is not as significant when asking specific questions. Because this project is looking more specifically at words related to Islam, I have decided not to remove advertisements to avoid making editorial changes to my corpora. 

It is essential to consider what changes, if any, should be made to a corpus, as these changes will affect the results. This project uses standard pre-processing conventions to clean the corpora prior to model training. These conventions include removing punctuation, numbers, and non-character tokens, and making every token lower case. While regularizing the spellings of certain words can make the results easier to interpret, it is still a manipulation that needs to be highlighted, and it is good practice to analyze results from an unmanipulated corpus as well. Manipulations can include the decision to remove advertisements, fix OCR errors, regularize the spellings of words, or even identify certain articles to omit.

One important step in my methodology was to create a shortlist of terms that would be most useful to better answer my research question. Creating queries around the word Muslim for the earlier chapters was not useful, for example, as this is too modern a term; instead, words like *mahomedan*, *mussulman*, and even *moslem* were more relevant to the time. It is also important to mention that spellings of these words were not as standardized at the time; for example, during this period *muhammadan, mahommedan, mohomedan, mahometan*, and *mohammedan* were all valid spellings of the term for Muslims in the mid-nineteenth century. The presence of *mohamedanism* and *mahomedans* (words used almost exclusively by non-Muslims) represent the positionality of the contributors of *The Times*. By defining the religion through the figure of Mohammed, these terms create a false analogy between the role of Jesus Christ in Christianity and Mohammed in Islam. Unlike in Christianity, where followers see Jesus Christ himself as a divine figure, Mohammed in Islam is a human prophet who shares the word of God. Therefore, the presence of these terms alone highlights the othering of Muslims within The Times. It was essential to create a different shortlist of query terms for each corpus, based on the popularity of terms during each individual period.

Different variations of words for Muslims were not the only aspect of my data that required consideration. Fixing OCR (Optical Character Recognition) errors, often called OCR noise, is another form of corpus manipulation especially relevant for this project. For example, the average accuracy of OCR for the 1856-1859 corpus was 81%. The accuracy of the OCR improves in relation to how modern the corpus is, in part because printing processes were much clearer and more standardized in 1914 than in 1856. Due to the accuracy range of 81%–88%, OCR noise does impact model performance. The kind of algorithms that word2vec uses, specifically skip-gram and Continuous Bag of Words (CBOW), tend to perform better in terms of resiliency to OCR noise when compared against other popular word embedding algorithms.  However, OCR noise remains a major challenge in text analysis of historical documents. 

There are multiple variations of words for Muslims (*mussulman* and *moslem* for example), but there were also many OCR (mis)spellings of these words (*mussulman* and *msuslman* for example). Word embedding models treat each misspelling as its own word (or vector). That meant there was a multitude of vectors created for every spelling instance for words for Muslims in the corpus, reducing the opportunities for the model to learn about the language of the corpus. To rectify this issue, I trained multiple models on the unmanipulated corpus first and then used these models to identify the top (mis)spellings of words for Muslims. This allowed me to return to the corpus itself and regularize all the spellings for these words using find/replace in BBEdit. For example, the word *mahomedan* had the following variations in the unmanipulated 1856–1859 corpus:*mlahomedan, miahomedan, mahomnedan, mahometan*, and *mahommedan*.  After using the trained models to identify all these instances, I then returned to the original corpus and regularized all these spellings to *mahomedan*. This meant the manipulated corpus considered all the varying instances as part of the same word vector, so it had a greater opportunity to understand the language.
