These quotations from *The Times* of London in 1989 highlight the intensity of British discourse around the Rushdie Affair in 1989, especially the ways Muslims in Britain were perceived not only as outsiders but also as belonging to an inherently violent and fanatical religion. In 1988, the Indian-born British author Salman Rushdie published a novel called *The Satanic Verses*, a magical-realist novel about two Indian Muslims living in England that included a revisionist—and uncomplimentary—subplot about the prophet Muhammed and the founding of Islam. Many Muslims around the world viewed the story in The Satanic Verses as scandalous, blasphemous, and deeply insulting, and the book was banned in India, Pakistan, and a number of majority-Muslim countries.  In 1989, some British Muslims organized public book burnings, and Iran’s Ayatollah Khomeini issued a fatwa calling for all devout Muslims to put Rushdie to death. In the aftermath of the Rushdie Affair, journalists and political commentators argued that it represented a clear break with the past. Now, they argued, the spread of fundamentalist interpretations of Islam that found expression in events like the Rushdie Affair were becoming a worldwide problem and, even more important, demonstrated that Islam was inherently incompatible with the supposedly “Western” values of tolerance and democracy. These beliefs undergird the anti-Muslim racism  that is the focus of this study.

In fact, these same journalists and commentators—reinforced later by scholars of literature, political science, and anthropology—argued that the Rushdie Affair should be seen as the starting point for the rise of contemporary anti-Muslim racism in Britain.  But while it is clear that the Rushdie Affair did spur anti-Muslim racism in Britain, historians have long understood that this is a phenomenon with much deeper roots in Britain’s colonial past. Anti-Muslim racism, in other words, was hardly new in the late twentieth century. Instead, it was already a recognized phenomenon at least by the time of the Indian Rebellion of 1857, and it periodically peaked over the next century in response to colonial events.  So far, however, the evidence for this continuity has largely been impressionistic or based on isolated examples, and the conclusions of historians have not shattered the popular narrative about a radical shift in anti-Muslim rhetoric following the Rushdie Affair. This dissertation seeks to change that by using digital text analysis methods and quantitative data to literally chart the development of anti-Muslim rhetoric in Britain from the mid-nineteenth century forward.

Using three case studies from 1857-59, 1898, and 1914, I use textual data to demonstrate that the British association between fanaticism and Islam was already well established long before the Rushdie Affair, and that the anti-Muslim rhetoric from that earlier period informed much of the modern language used in debates over Islam and the West. This dissertation is the first historical interpretation to use digital text analysis (specifically word embedding models) to quantitatively explore the idea that anti-Muslim sentiments in the United Kingdom are not recent but instead are at least two centuries old. Habitual references to the so-called fanaticism of Muslims during the Rushdie Affair in fact originated in the language of the colonial “civilizing mission,” which created hierarchies of savagery amongst colonial populations to justify their subjugation to the British. Part of the reason these narratives have been so enduring in Britain was due to the centrality of the imperial project to British popular culture.
