> “In the eyes of fellow Muslims, apostasy from his faith is the most serious crime a Muslim can commit. The second most serious crime is to insult the prophet Muhammad. Put them together and they constitute the sort of challenge which will drive fanatical Muslims to extremes.” 

## Background
The ‘Rushdie Affair’ (or ‘*The Satanic Verses* affair’) refers to the international controversy in 1989 surrounding the writer Salman Rushdie. Rushdie was a successful essayist and novelist, born in colonial Bombay in 1947 before migrating to Britain. On September 26 1988, he published his fourth novel *The Satanic Verses*, which explored the experience of British immigrants from former colonies, in this case from colonial India. Rushdie painted a portrait of Islam (and the Prophet Muhammad) in a postmodern satirical style, including a visit to a brothel named Hijab in which the twelve sex workers use the names of the twelve wives of the Prophet Muhammad as a business tactic.  The book was seen by many Muslims around the world as scandalous, blasphemous, and deeply insulting. India became the first country to formally ban *The Satanic Verses* on October 5, 1988, and by the end of November it had also been banned in Bangladesh, Sudan, and South Africa. 

Many Muslims in Britain, where the book was first published, shared these reactions to Rushdie’s novel. British Muslims began publicly demonstrating against the novel shortly after India banned it.  By the end of October, the British Muslim current affairs magazine *Impact International* issued a call for Penguin Books (the publisher) to withdraw the book and to issue a formal apology to Muslims.  A demonstration in the town of Bolton on December 2 was attended by 7,000 people and saw the first public burning of the novel. Although this event was largely ignored by the national press, a larger—and better publicized—demonstration took place in the city of Bradford in January 1989. Bradford was home to the second largest concentration of Muslims in Britain at the time, behind Birmingham. The January 14 rally in Bradford in fact turned out to be much smaller than the one in Bolton, drawing only 1,000-2,000 attendees, but because of the publicity around it the reports and photographs of the novel being burned made international headlines. The public burning of *The Satanic Verses* in Bradford on January 14, 1989, quite literally fanned the flames of the British anti-Muslim rhetoric. 

On February 14, 1989, Iran’s Ayatollah Khomeini declared Rushdie guilty of apostasy, or religious sedition, and sentenced him to death in a fatwa. The Ayatollah’s proclamation, broadcasted on Iranian radio, called on “all valiant Muslims wherever they may be in the world to kill them [Rushdie, the editors, and publishers] without delay, so that no one will dare insult the sacred beliefs of Muslims henceforth.”  The proclamation was undergirded by the assumption, on the part of both the Ayatollah and Western commentators, that all Muslims everywhere prioritized their Muslim identities over their other identities and that the Shi’a Ayatollah of Iran spoke for all Muslims.  While most British Muslims did not take this fatwa seriously and saw it instead as an example of Iranian fanaticism, many still found the book deeply offensive and argued it should be suppressed.  These demands and demonstrations caused debates over Islam’s compatibility with British values, especially free speech. Meanwhile the Iranian fatwa, though largely dismissed by British Muslims, was seized upon by British media as an example of the inherent violence and fanaticism of Islam. This informed many of the narratives found in *The Times* that set British Muslims outside the bounds of Britishness. The prevailing idea was that these so-called religious fanatics were incompatible with British values, specifically free speech. 

As it happened, the Rushdie Affair occurred during a very tumultuous year for the international order. The Berlin Wall fell several months after the fatwa was issued, in November 1989, marking the end of communist control in Eastern Europe and heralding the collapse of the Soviet Union two years later, in 1991. The end of the communist threat, which had been the dominant feature of the postwar order in Europe and the world, just happened to coincide with a huge international controversy over the place of Islam in the West caused by the Rushdie Affair. This event was then used as evidence for the belief that Islam represented the greatest threat to the West. With the communists quelled the “newest” threat to Western order returned to the old enemy of Islam.
