There is no shortage of historical works that make a case for the intensity of anti-Muslim rhetoric from the British during this rebellion.  I chose the Rebellion because it was an explosive event involving Muslims that converged with the expansion of the press. I use the first case study to test both the efficacy of the methods and the validity of the existing historiographic arguments. The existing historiography makes such a strong case that the Rebellion was a significant moment when the language about Muslims changed to be much more associated with fanaticism and violence that it can be used in this context to prove that the digital methods are working effectively.

The 1857 Rebellion looms large in both the historiography of colonial India and of the British Empire more generally.  The Uprising (from May 1857 until November 1858) was a rebellion of the Bengal Army against the rule of the British East India Company. It was incredibly violent, resulting in over 100,000 Indians and around 6,000 British deaths.  The Rebellion was the most serious military challenge to the British Empire during the nineteenth century, and as such has continued to hold a place of primacy within the histories of both South Asia and of British colonialism.  There are also many primary sources available on the Rebellion. 
During the nineteenth century, India was the largest and most profitable colony in the British Empire. The discourse in the British press surrounding the Rebellion was shaped by the belief that the Empire could not afford to lose control of India. British military officers were increasingly involved in the use of the media to drive public opinion during this event, playing a formative role in the shaping of British popular culture for the rest of the nineteenth century.  Many of the articles in The Times that discuss the Rebellion were written by military officials who were also serving as war correspondents. 

The Rebellion began in early 1857, when a rumor spread amongst the soldiers (sepoys) in the Indian Army that their new Enfield rifles required ammunition that went against the religious beliefs of both Hindus and Muslims. Specifically, the rumor claimed that the cartridges to be used in the new rifles were greased with a combination of lard (pig fat) and tallow (cow fat). Soldiers had to bite open the cartridges to load these cartridges into the rifles. Hinduism does not allow for the consumption of beef, while Islam does not allow for the consumption of pork. The idea that sepoys would have to forsake their religious beliefs to serve an alien colonial power did not sit well with the Indian soldiers. Some even interpreted it as part of a proselytizing plot to convert them to Christianity.  Although this rumor of greased cartridges was the spark that ignited the Rebellion, it was the proverbial straw that broke the camel’s back, as sepoys were dissatisfied with conditions in the Indian Army for many other reasons having to do with pay, travel, and the growing footprint of British rule in the subcontinent. However, Britons seized on the issues of the cartridges, which lent a distinctly religious character to discussions and depictions of the event.
            
What we know is this: on April 23, 1857, Lieutenant Colonel George Carmichael-Smyth of the 3rd Bengal Light Cavalry announced a parade for April 24 in Meerut where the 90 men of his regiment were expected to fire their new rifles in a training drill and all but five refused. The next day, these men were imprisoned and sentenced to ten years of hard labor for insubordination.  Soldiers from other regiments quickly formed a plan to free the initial dissenters, which swiftly turned into open revolt. From there, the Rebellion quickly spread from Meerut throughout north-central India, and the soldiers were joined by thousands of peasants in a struggle that threatened to destroy British colonial power on the Indian subcontinent. The Bengal Army, which garrisoned Bengal and North India, was at the center of this event—sixty-nine of its seventy-four regiments rebelled.  Within a few days of the initial event, the rebels had taken control of Delhi—the center of the East India Company’s control. On May 30, 1857, rebels laid siege to the British garrison of Lucknow. The early months of the Rebellion saw much of north-central India coming under rebel control. 

The British resorted to extremely violent measures to quell the Rebellion. The initial success of the rebels led many British officers to participate in violent acts of retribution again both sepoys and Indian civilians.  At its start the Rebellion was seen by the British as a plot of the Dharma Sabha of Calcutta, an organization established to preserve Hinduism from the social reforms of the British. However, this idea of a ‘religious plot’ soon shifted and came to be identified as a Muslim conspiracy—a perception that has continued into the present.  

The most well-known and widely reported incident of the Rebellion occurred on June 27, 1857, and is known as the Cawnpore Massacre.  The Rebellion reached Cawnpore, an important British garrison, on June 5, 1857. The members of the British army stationed there agreed to surrender on the condition that they and their families would be guaranteed safe passage on boats heading to Allahabad.  As the approximately four hundred and fifty British men, women and children stationed at Cawnpore made their way to the boats, the rebels ambushed them and set the boats on fire. More than half of the Britons were killed in and around the boats on June 27, with the survivors being mostly women and children. Of the two hundred or so women and children who survived, more died in the following two weeks from sickness, while the remainder were held captive in a palace known as the Bibighar, or “House of the Ladies.”  Then, a little more than two weeks later, on July 15, the remaining one hundred and twenty-five British women and children being kept in the Bibighar were stabbed to death and their bodies were flung down a well.  

The violence at Cawnpore was not random and was likely in response to the brutal acts of retribution the British officers undertook in the early months of the Rebellion.  Nevertheless, Cawnpore quickly became the rallying call for the British for the remainder of the Rebellion. After the deaths at Cawnpore, British contributors to The Times at home wanted retribution. The British response when retaking Cawnpore was equally savage. British policy made no distinction between insurgent soldiers and local people who remained loyal or were simply not involved.  The British press blamed Muslims for the violence at Cawnpore, which was reflected in the rhetoric. In fact, the events at Cawnpore led the British public to accept, and in some cases even celebrate, the acts of violence perpetrated by the British soldiers for the duration of the Rebellion. 

While the rebels had early success, the tide began to turn in Fall 1857. The rebels held their stronghold in Delhi through the summer against repeated sieges, but the city was successfully recaptured by the British on September 21, 1857. British forces arrived to relieve the besieged garrison of Lucknow shortly after the Cawnpore massacre in the middle of July. The siege at Lucknow was successfully broken on November 17, 1857, though combat continued in pockets for several months. By the end of 1857, however, most Britons were assured of their eventual victory, and British forces successfully retook in Lucknow in March 1858.  Although the last rebels surrendered in November 1859, by November 1858 the Rebellion was functionally over. This event was also significant as it marked the end of Company rule.
