Following the establishment of a defensive alliance with Germany in August 1914, the leaders of the Ottoman Empire hoped that Germany would win the war before it became necessary to join it.  The belief on both sides at the start of the First World War was that it would be over very quickly—but it was clear after the German defeat on the Marne in early September 1914 that the war, especially on the Western front, would not be won quickly. This led the Germans to put increased pressure on the Ottomans to formally join the war effort. Ottoman leaders acquiesced to these demands in late October and ordered an attack on Russian naval installations in the Black Sea. However, the Ottomans presented a different narrative to the world, which served a purpose for their eventual declaration on November 11. The Ottomans maintained that the Russians had started the attacks, and the Ottomans were simply defending themselves.  Meanwhile, a broad consensus about the meaning of jihad had grown amongst Muslim scholars during this period. They argued that when an Islamic state was under attack, fighting was seen as an individual duty incumbent on every Muslim.  By maintaining that Russia started the attacks, the Ottomans relied on this understanding of jihad as both justification and a call to action. 
    
On November 11, the proclamation of jihad and associated fatwa were agreed upon by the Ottoman Chamber of Deputies and was officially announced on November 14.  German officials believed that encouraging Muslims to rebel against the Allied nations would be an effective war strategy. Although there were German efforts to stir discontent among Muslims across all the Allied colonies, there is evidence that German politicians identified Britian Muslims, and British Indian Muslims in particular, as a special concern.  The dominant narrative at the time around this proclamation was that Germany heavily influenced and encouraged the Ottomans to do so. The evidence demonstrates that this was an Ottoman decision, and not a German ploy, but that was not the prevailing wisdom at the time.  British newspapers quickly reported on the role of Germany’s Kaiser in this declaration. *The Times* republished a telegram sent from Kaiser Wilhelm to the German Crown Prince on November 19, which read: 
>To his Imperial Highness the German Crown Prince—Sheikh-ul-Islam has published a Fetwa according to which every Musulman must, as a religious duty, fight to the uttermost against England, Russia, and France, the oppressors of Islam. This Fetwa will be distributed throughout the entire Mahomedan world, and is now proclaimed to the pilgrims in Mecca. This means the Holy War for the whole Islamic world. 

The influence of Germany on this declaration is most famously exemplified through the January 1915 publication of Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje’s article, or manifesto, entitled “Heilige Oorlog [Holy War] Made in Germany.” Hurgronje was a Dutch expert on Islam and served as an advisor to the colonial government of the Dutch East Indies. In this article Hurgronje attacked Germany’s cadre of orientalist scholars and accuses them of being the instigators behind the Ottoman jihad proclamation.  His article was immediately translated into English and gained significant traction in academic debates throughout the West, essentially defining the way it was understood by Britons. 
    
In practice, the Ottoman declaration of jihad was not effective as a war strategy; it did not lead to the widespread uprising of Muslims around the world. British commentators at the time had no way to predict the effects of such a declaration and were thus deeply concerned about the effect it would have on their Muslim subjects. At the time of the declaration, the British Empire claimed the largest number of Muslims in the world, with almost half a million Muslims serving in the Indian Army alone.  As one commentator from The Times put it, “A certain amount of anxiety is mingled with indignation…Though a holy war is unlikely, the appearance of Turkey as a belligerent will undoubtedly stir up British Mahomedan subjects.”  
    
There were a variety of reasons why the call to jihad did not result in widespread uprisings from colonized Muslims, such as the fact that many Muslims were simply not prepared to revolt and join the Ottoman Empire. The risks associated with uprising also stopped many from responding to this call. In the British Army, the punishment for sedition was death. As said, there was a particular interest in stirring revolt amongst Muslims in the British army from the Germans and Ottomans. However, Muslims in the British army were unlikely to risk their lives without seeing evidence of the jihad’s success. Due to the lack of any large-scale response to the declaration, the benefits of rebelling did not outweigh the costs (their lives). On top of this, many Muslims did not accept the authority of the Ottoman caliph to speak for the ‘Muslim world.’

Although the Muslims of the British Empire did not take up the call to jihad during the war, British officials were deeply worried, and some of this worry was expressed in British newspapers. Indeed, even though the call to jihad had little effect, the British reaction to it nevertheless revealed their belief that Muslims prioritized their religion above all else and would thus respond to a call to jihad unquestionably. As a result, anti-Muslim language in British newspapers spiked in this period, as it had whenever imperial events involving Muslims occurred since the 1857 Rebellion. Although the level of this anti-Muslim language did not reach the heights that it had in the Rebellion or during Omdurman/Candia, when the lives of British women and children were clearly at stake, the Ottoman call to jihad did results in the emergence of a new word—extremist—as a short-hand for anti-Muslim bigotry in British popular culture, joining older terms like fanatic and fanaticism. After extensive close reading in *The Times*, it appears that the Ottoman declaration of jihad did not intensify existing anti-Muslim rhetoric so much as confirmed it.
