I chose these three case studies because they have been identified by other historians as significant moments in the construction of the British perception of Muslims as inherently ‘violent’ and ‘fanatical’. These were also moments of insecurity for the British Empire, in varying degrees, and I demonstrate that British rhetoric was more vitriolic in moments of insecurity. Islam, and the public perception of it, played a significant role in all these events, and, more importantly, these events also spurred significant discursive activity that reveals the way Britons themselves talked about Islam. 

##### Sepoy Rebellion
My first case study is the 1857 Rebellion in colonial India. The Rebellion, sometimes referred to as the Indian Mutiny or the Sepoy Mutiny, was a major uprising against the rule of the British East India Company, and ran from May 1857 until November 1858. This was a watershed moment in the history of the British press and anti-Muslim rhetoric. The Rebellion was the second international and first colonial event, after the Crimean War, to receive widespread coverage in a markedly expanding British press. This occurred concurrently with a stark reduction in taxes on printing, meaning more Britons were reading newspapers in the mid nineteenth century than ever before. The frequent attention in the British press given to the Rebellion made it a salient issue for both contemporary Britons and historians of the British Empire. Ilyse Morgenstein Fuerst argues that the Rebellion was the moment that Muslims became defined by the British as essentially religious actors.  The way the British categorized religion in India dramatically changed how religion—as a category—was applied to people across the British Empire and within the borders of the metropole. Religion, along with race, became a central node for how the British classified colonized people in the Empire. Specifically, the Rebellion was a crucial event in the racialization of Muslims as a group under imperial classifications. 

There is no shortage of historical works that make a case for the intensity of anti-Muslim rhetoric from the British during this rebellion.  I chose the Rebellion because it was an explosive event involving Muslims that converged with the expansion of the press. I use the first case study to test both the efficacy of the methods and the validity of the existing historiographic arguments. The existing historiography makes such a strong case that the Rebellion was a significant moment when the language about Muslims changed to be much more associated with fanaticism and violence that it can be used in this context to prove that the digital methods are working effectively.

The 1857 Rebellion looms large in both the historiography of colonial India and of the British Empire more generally.  The Uprising (from May 1857 until November 1858) was a rebellion of the Bengal Army against the rule of the British East India Company. It was incredibly violent, resulting in over 100,000 Indians and around 6,000 British deaths.  The Rebellion was the most serious military challenge to the British Empire during the nineteenth century, and as such has continued to hold a place of primacy within the histories of both South Asia and of British colonialism.  There are also many primary sources available on the Rebellion. 
During the nineteenth century, India was the largest and most profitable colony in the British Empire. The discourse in the British press surrounding the Rebellion was shaped by the belief that the Empire could not afford to lose control of India. British military officers were increasingly involved in the use of the media to drive public opinion during this event, playing a formative role in the shaping of British popular culture for the rest of the nineteenth century.  Many of the articles in The Times that discuss the Rebellion were written by military officials who were also serving as war correspondents. 

The Rebellion began in early 1857, when a rumor spread amongst the soldiers (sepoys) in the Indian Army that their new Enfield rifles required ammunition that went against the religious beliefs of both Hindus and Muslims. Specifically, the rumor claimed that the cartridges to be used in the new rifles were greased with a combination of lard (pig fat) and tallow (cow fat). Soldiers had to bite open the cartridges to load these cartridges into the rifles. Hinduism does not allow for the consumption of beef, while Islam does not allow for the consumption of pork. The idea that sepoys would have to forsake their religious beliefs to serve an alien colonial power did not sit well with the Indian soldiers. Some even interpreted it as part of a proselytizing plot to convert them to Christianity.  Although this rumor of greased cartridges was the spark that ignited the Rebellion, it was the proverbial straw that broke the camel’s back, as sepoys were dissatisfied with conditions in the Indian Army for many other reasons having to do with pay, travel, and the growing footprint of British rule in the subcontinent. However, Britons seized on the issues of the cartridges, which lent a distinctly religious character to discussions and depictions of the event.
            
What we know is this: on April 23, 1857, Lieutenant Colonel George Carmichael-Smyth of the 3rd Bengal Light Cavalry announced a parade for April 24 in Meerut where the 90 men of his regiment were expected to fire their new rifles in a training drill and all but five refused. The next day, these men were imprisoned and sentenced to ten years of hard labor for insubordination.  Soldiers from other regiments quickly formed a plan to free the initial dissenters, which swiftly turned into open revolt. From there, the Rebellion quickly spread from Meerut throughout north-central India, and the soldiers were joined by thousands of peasants in a struggle that threatened to destroy British colonial power on the Indian subcontinent. The Bengal Army, which garrisoned Bengal and North India, was at the center of this event—sixty-nine of its seventy-four regiments rebelled.  Within a few days of the initial event, the rebels had taken control of Delhi—the center of the East India Company’s control. On May 30, 1857, rebels laid siege to the British garrison of Lucknow. The early months of the Rebellion saw much of north-central India coming under rebel control. 

The British resorted to extremely violent measures to quell the Rebellion. The initial success of the rebels led many British officers to participate in violent acts of retribution again both sepoys and Indian civilians.  At its start the Rebellion was seen by the British as a plot of the Dharma Sabha of Calcutta, an organization established to preserve Hinduism from the social reforms of the British. However, this idea of a ‘religious plot’ soon shifted and came to be identified as a Muslim conspiracy—a perception that has continued into the present.  

The most well-known and widely reported incident of the Rebellion occurred on June 27, 1857, and is known as the Cawnpore Massacre.  The Rebellion reached Cawnpore, an important British garrison, on June 5, 1857. The members of the British army stationed there agreed to surrender on the condition that they and their families would be guaranteed safe passage on boats heading to Allahabad.  As the approximately four hundred and fifty British men, women and children stationed at Cawnpore made their way to the boats, the rebels ambushed them and set the boats on fire. More than half of the Britons were killed in and around the boats on June 27, with the survivors being mostly women and children. Of the two hundred or so women and children who survived, more died in the following two weeks from sickness, while the remainder were held captive in a palace known as the Bibighar, or “House of the Ladies.”  Then, a little more than two weeks later, on July 15, the remaining one hundred and twenty-five British women and children being kept in the Bibighar were stabbed to death and their bodies were flung down a well.  

The violence at Cawnpore was not random and was likely in response to the brutal acts of retribution the British officers undertook in the early months of the Rebellion.  Nevertheless, Cawnpore quickly became the rallying call for the British for the remainder of the Rebellion. After the deaths at Cawnpore, British contributors to The Times at home wanted retribution. The British response when retaking Cawnpore was equally savage. British policy made no distinction between insurgent soldiers and local people who remained loyal or were simply not involved.  The British press blamed Muslims for the violence at Cawnpore, which was reflected in the rhetoric. In fact, the events at Cawnpore led the British public to accept, and in some cases even celebrate, the acts of violence perpetrated by the British soldiers for the duration of the Rebellion. 

While the rebels had early success, the tide began to turn in Fall 1857. The rebels held their stronghold in Delhi through the summer against repeated sieges, but the city was successfully recaptured by the British on September 21, 1857. British forces arrived to relieve the besieged garrison of Lucknow shortly after the Cawnpore massacre in the middle of July. The siege at Lucknow was successfully broken on November 17, 1857, though combat continued in pockets for several months. By the end of 1857, however, most Britons were assured of their eventual victory, and British forces successfully retook in Lucknow in March 1858.  Although the last rebels surrendered in November 1859, by November 1858 the Rebellion was functionally over. This event was also significant as it marked the end of Company rule.  


##### 1898 
My second case study moves forward forty years to the 1898 Battle of Omdurman, which occurred during the British conquest of Sudan in the late nineteenth century. The battle, on September 2, 1898, was a part of the successful British colonial conquest of the Sudan. The historiography on Omdurman indicated that the battle precipitated an increase in anti-Muslim rhetoric in Britain.  I chose Omdurman not because it was unique, but because it is representative of the period of High Imperialism (1880–1914). The public interest in empires increased during this period, with substantial reporting in British newspapers focused on events in the Empire and the world. I could have chosen any number of events involving Muslims from this period and gotten similar results in the analysis. Due to the data constraints of digital text analysis, I am unable to focus on the entire period from 1857–1915. That is why I chose case studies that had been identified as connected to anti-Muslim thought, while also seeking to select case studies that occurred at different times throughout the period. I chose case studies that span the geographic reach of the empire, to obtain a more complete picture of how events of the colonial period shaped British perceptions of Islam.

In the first week of September 1898 two events occurred in the colonial periphery that strongly influenced British discourse on Muslims: the battle of Omdurman and the Candia Massacre. The Battle of Omdurman occurred on September 2, 1898, during the British reconquest of Sudan, which coincided with the flourishing of military journalism in Britain. The Mahdist Islamic State was established in Sudan in 1885, overthrowing the British-controlled Khedivate of Egypt. Following their removal by the Mahdists, the British sought to consolidate control of the region to safeguard the Suez Canal and aimed to recapture the Sudan. On September 2, 1898, the British successfully retook Omdurman after a brief and unequal battle with the Mahdist state, overwhelming the Mahdist army with superior military technology. Then on September 6, four days after the battle of Omdurman, the Candia massacre occurred on the island of Crete. In response to the actions of Cretan Christians during the Cretan revolt in 1897, Muslim irregulars massacred local Christians in the city of Candia. Between 500 and 800 Christians were killed, as well as 14 British military personnel.  This event accelerated the end of Ottoman rule on Crete, with the autonomous Cretan State governing the island from 1898 through 1913. Both events were violent, but the violence in Omdurman was planned while in Candia it was spontaneous. Although the two events were unrelated and occurred 1430 miles apart, because they occurred so chronologically close together British newspapers treated them as related phenomena and reported on them extensively. This chapter argues that the discourse around these two events shaped British perceptions of Islam, as fanatical and incompatible with Western values in important ways at the end of the nineteenth century. I also argue for the need for a world historical perspective when considering issues of public perception. Importantly, I demonstrate how digital text analysis can bring to light previously hidden connections between world events.

###### Battle of Omdurman
With the establishment of the Suez Canal in 1869, the strategic importance of Egypt and the Sudan grew exponentially more significant to British imperial administrators because it afforded a much shorter route to British India. The British occupied both Egypt and the Sudan in 1882 and established a protectorate over the region. Unlike a formal colony whose internal affairs were controlled by the colonial power, protectorates were nominally independent states whose external affairs were controlled by the colonial power. In both Egypt and the Sudan, the government was continued to be staffed with Turkish and Egyptian administrators, maintaining a formal connection to the Ottoman Empire (with British influence). In the Sudan, Muhammad Ahmad ibn al-Sayyid Abd Allah, later dubbed al Mahdi (Arabic for “Guided one” or “Promised One”), began a religious movement in 1880, called the Mahdist movement, which aimed to expel the Egyptian and Turkish officials running the government and restore Islam to its original purity. This movement grew in size and influence, and the Mahdi’s army successfully took Khartoum in early 1885 after about a year of conflict with the Ottoman-Egyptian administration. The Mahdi created a theocratic state in Sudan (the goal of his movement) and established Omdurman as the new capital of the Mahdist state the same year. Although the Mahdi died in 1885, he was succeeded by his Khalifa Abd Allah. The Khalifa built many public institutions, including mosques, a prison, and markets in Omdurman. The population of the city boomed from around 30,000 people in 1885 to over 200,000 by the end of the 1890s.

The Mahdist rebels expelled the British influenced Ottoman-Egyptian administration from the Sudan in 1885. The British worried about the implications of the Mahdist state right from the start of its creation, fearing that Mahdism would spread to Egypt and threaten their control of the Suez Canal. In the years following 1885, British propaganda focused on misery in the Sudan under Mahdist rule, made popular by works like Joseph Ohrwalder’s *Ten Year’s Captivity in the Mahdi’s Camp* and Rudolf Slatin’s *Fire and Sword in Sudan*.  Both works were written by men who had been captured during the Mahdist revolt in 1885 and were held captive for a decade. The purpose of this propaganda was to emphasize the flourishing of Egypt under the British protectorate, in contrast to the so-called languishing of the Mahdist regime. Both accounts were widely read by the British public, prompting widespread public interest in the colonial conflicts in Egypt and the Sudan. Slatin became an honorary member of the Royal Victorian Order in 1896 and served as an officer on the staff of the Egyptian army in the battle of Omdurman. 

Herbert Kitchener, a British Army colonel and colonial administrator, played a central role in the reconstruction of the Egyptian Army under British tutelage.  He won national fame spearheading the campaign to retake the Sudan, which culminated in the Battle of Omdurman. The first phase of this conquest began in June 1898, but the combined Anglo-Egyptian advance took off in earnest on August 24, 1898. The battle of Omdurman occurred a little more than a week later, on September 1 and 2.  The first day of the battle began with the British shelling the city of Omdurman, which included the use of a new explosive called lyddite that was used to destroy the tomb where the Mahdi has been buried. The following day, September 2, armed combat began between the Anglo-Egyptian Army and the Mahdist Army. The technological disparity between the British-led army and the Khalifa’s army was vast, making for a very unequal fight. The battle itself was over in a little more than 5 hours. By its conclusion, British forces had killed 11,000, wounded 16,000 and taken 4,000 soldiers as prisoners; Anglo-Egyptian losses were a combined 500 dead and wounded.  

New military technology, especially the machine gun, played a major role in the British victory. Although the Anglo-Egyptian army was vastly outnumbered, the superiority of its weaponry made the fight extremely uneven. Kitchener’s army was composed of around 17,000 Egyptian and Sudanese troops, as well as 8,200 British troops, while the Khalifa’s army consisted of between 30,000 and 60,000 troops .  Nearly half of the Khalifa’s army was killed or wounded during the battle, while the Anglo-Egyptian losses were a combined 500 dead and wounded. It is important to emphasize that the defeat was not due to a ‘primitivity’ of the Mahdist forces, as argued by the British forces, but rather that the tactics and strategies of the Khalifa were more fit for the weaponry of the 1880s and had not been adjusted in the face of new rapid-fire machine guns.  Needless to say, the battle was an overwhelming victory for the British forces, and its success sealed the political future of the Sudan and British control of the Nile. 

*The Times* coverage of the events in Omdurman featured significant discussion of Islam. Contributors to British newspapers characterized soldiers of the Mahdist army during the battle by their “fanatical courage.” An eyewitness from the battle reported: “there is no instance in history of a more superb devotion to a cause, or a greater contempt for death….the carnage was fearful, as the dauntless fanatics hurled themselves to inevitable death”  But even though British newspapers portrayed the dervishes as religious fanatics blindly devoted to their cause, the actual motivations of the soldiers in the Khalifa’s army were likely based as much on fear as they were on commitment to Islam. Omdurman was governed on a military command structure, where political leaders were also military commanders. In most instances, this military command structure derived from preexisting social hierarchies, with the Khalifa and his brothers having the most power.  These military structures left little room for rebellion and created a strict social climate in Omdurman where rule breakers were quickly apprehended and punished. In fact, many of the Khalifa’s soldiers acted not only out of a sense of religious obligation but also because they had no other choice. 

###### Candia Massacre
I built the corpus for this chapter with the battle of Omdurman in mind as the case study event, which is why it covers January 1, 1897, through December 31, 1899. Yet while reading through *The Times* articles published during the battle of Omdurman, it became clear that another contemporary event—the Candia Massacre—also shaped British discourse around Muslims. Four days after the battle of Omdurman, Cretan Muslims rose up and killed hundreds of Christians on the island, including British officers and civilians. London newspapers, including The Times, provided extensive coverage of this event and also discussed it in concert with the battle of Omdurman. For example, The Pall Mall Gazette and The Times noted that British causalities in Crete were almost as high in raw numbers as those suffered by General Kitchener’s army in Omdurman.  
    
It is important to note that while the British were heavily involved in Crete during this period, it remained a part of the Ottoman Empire until the end of 1898. The significance of this event on British rhetoric is an example of the interconnectedness of the world by the end of the nineteenth century, as well as an example of what is lost when doing history with rigid boundaries and taxonomies. 
    
The Candia Massacre was just one event in the bloody struggle over the future of Crete at the end of the nineteenth century. Crete—a Mediterranean island off the coast of Greece and Turkey—became a part of the Ottoman Empire in 1646. By the end of the nineteenth century, the island had two major religious groups: Christians and Muslims. In 1895, a Christian rebellion, inspired by a desire to rejoin Greece and transform Cretan society into one where Christians would be dominant, broke out against Ottoman rule.  The Ottomans struggled to end the violent revolt, leading to the involvement of Great Britain and France in 1896. To end the fighting, the consuls of Britain and France proposed a new constitution for Crete to both the Ottomans and the revolutionary assembly. The new constitution was accepted by the rebelling Christians and the Ottomans, bringing about an end to fighting in August 1896. Among other things, it called for the Ottomans to appoint a Christian governor of Crete and to reserve twice as many jobs for Christians as for Muslims.  As part of this restructuring of Cretan society, the Christian rebels forcefully evicted many Cretan Muslims from their homes.  The majority of Muslims took refuge in Candia (now Heraklion) where a 25 square mile military cordon was established by the British forces.  

In January 1897, skirmishes began again between Cretan Christians and Muslims. The new constitution of Crete increased sectarian violence across the island. At the end of January, Eleftherios Venizelos, a well-known Cretan political figure, and other Cretan Christians established a base in the Akrotiri peninsula, including the city of Chania, and proclaimed the annexation of the island by Grece.  This proclamation was quickly followed by the raising of the Greek flag in the Akrotiri peninsula. The revolt was not localized to the western side of the island (which included the Akrotiri peninsula) and quickly spread to the Eastern city of Sitia. The revolt was especially violent in Sitia, with Christian insurgents plundering Muslim homes and systematically surrounding the city’s Muslim population. The insurgents set all the mosques on fire, killing most of the Muslims who had gathered in them. By the end of the revolt, the total number of Muslims killed in Sitia was estimated to be 2,500 (according to Pınar Ȿenıȿık, only 25 of the city’s Muslims managed to escape the slaughter). 

The Greek state attempted to intervene in February, and began supplying the insurgents with warships and troops.  By mid-February, Greece had occupied the island and issued a proclamation announcing the annexation of Crete. The European powers demanded a ceasefire and sent a British-led peacekeeping mission to the island in March. As part of this mission, Great Britain took responsibility for supervising the proposed reforms and maintaining peace, as well for protecting the welfare of the Muslim community in Candia. The British sought to maintain the status quo, which including allowing the Christians who had taken the homes of Muslims in the villages to stay. 

The Muslim refugees that had been forced into Candia were angry with the policy of the British admirals, specifically the lack of protection to return to their villages. A contributor to The Times reported that Muslims were unable to visit their villages safely “because their Christian fellow countrymen would cut their throats if they attempted to retake possession of their homes and lands in the interior.”  On September 6, 1898, Muslims held a demonstration in the Place d’Armes to express their dissatisfaction.  The demonstration began peacefully until the British authorities stationed officials of the Cretan Assembly (which only represented the Christian portion of the population) in the tithe office with British army forces as protection. This angered the Muslim demonstrators who began trying to force their way into the tithe office. British army forces then began to fire on them, causing the Muslim demonstrators to turn their ire onto local Christians. In the resulting melee, they killed between 500 and 800 people. The event was described in detail in The Times the day after the event.

>A large crowd of unarmed Mahomedans gathered and tried to force their way into the building. As they persisted in their attempts the troops were at last compelled to fire on them. Greatly enraged, the Mahomedans at once ran off for their arms, attacked the British detachment, and rushed through the Christian quarter of the town, firing at persons they saw at the windows, and setting fire to houses. 

The violence only ended when British warships in the harbor began a sustained bombardment of Candia.  During the mayhem, the local British Vice-Consul, Lysimachus Andrew Calocherino, and his family were burned alive in their home during the massacre.  This event was widely reported in The Times and other British newspapers and used to highlight the so-called barbarity of Muslims. In response to this event, the major European powers demanded the withdrawal of Ottoman troops from Crete within a month. Two months after the massacre, on November 28, Ottoman troops left Crete, marking the end of the Ottoman presence and the establishment of an autonomous state of Crete. 

While the battle of Omdurman and the massacre at Candia were not directly connected, they became connected by the way they were reported in British newspapers. Many British newspapers discussed them in concert and noted that the fatalities in Candia had been as least as severe as those suffered in the battle of Omdurman. As the Pall-Mall Gazette put it: 

>Further particulars of the outbreak at Candia make it perfectly clear that the affair was much more serious even than it was at first reported to be–which is saying a good deal. There has been something very like a general massacre of Christians, the British Vice-Consul has been burned to death in his own house, and the loss of British soldiers killed and wounded is just about equal to that sustained by the 21st Lancers in their brilliant charge at Omdurman. 

The way British newspapers reported on these two events allowed British newspaper readers to draw a clear connection between them. A report on the events in Candia from *The Times* framed the events in relation to those in Omdurman: “In the midst of our rejoicings over the victory which paves the way for civilization under British auspices in the heart of Africa, the outbreak which took place in Candia yesterday reminds us how troublesome it is to carry out the simplest piece of work in conjunction with that remarkable instrument the Concert of Europe.”  The Concert of Europe, which existed roughly from 1815 to 1914, refers to the agreement between the great powers of Europe to maintain the balance of power. The British presence in Crete was part of this larger European geopolitical order of the nineteenth century, underscoring the importance of taking a world historical perspective. The Empire did not exist in isolation from the surrounding world, and this is highlighted by the importance of events in Crete in British newspapers. 

##### German-Ottoman Jihad
My third case study, the Ottoman proclamation of jihad in 1914, was a global event that caused significant concern to the British state due to the high number of colonized Muslims serving in the British armies during the First World War. The Ottoman proclamation primarily targeted Muslim subjects in the British, French, and Russia Empires, and called upon them to resist their oppressors.  Some of the literature views the event in terms of its role in the development of anti-colonialism and Pan-Islamism. Dietrich Jung argues that the declaration should be viewed in the context of the rise of Pan-Islamism,  which reached a peak in the mid-twentieth century.  The declaration of jihad underscores the way that anti-colonialism and anti-imperialism in this period attained a distinctly Islamic character, which is one of the main reasons I selected this event as a case study. This case study interrogates how the widespread fear of a worldwide jihad shaped and influenced the British discourse on Islam in such a global context. 

Following the establishment of a defensive alliance with Germany in August 1914, the leaders of the Ottoman Empire hoped that Germany would win the war before it became necessary to join it.  The belief on both sides at the start of the First World War was that it would be over very quickly—but it was clear after the German defeat on the Marne in early September 1914 that the war, especially on the Western front, would not be won quickly. This led the Germans to put increased pressure on the Ottomans to formally join the war effort. Ottoman leaders acquiesced to these demands in late October and ordered an attack on Russian naval installations in the Black Sea. However, the Ottomans presented a different narrative to the world, which served a purpose for their eventual declaration on November 11. The Ottomans maintained that the Russians had started the attacks, and the Ottomans were simply defending themselves.  Meanwhile, a broad consensus about the meaning of jihad had grown amongst Muslim scholars during this period. They argued that when an Islamic state was under attack, fighting was seen as an individual duty incumbent on every Muslim.  By maintaining that Russia started the attacks, the Ottomans relied on this understanding of jihad as both justification and a call to action. 
    
On November 11, the proclamation of jihad and associated fatwa were agreed upon by the Ottoman Chamber of Deputies and was officially announced on November 14.  German officials believed that encouraging Muslims to rebel against the Allied nations would be an effective war strategy. Although there were German efforts to stir discontent among Muslims across all the Allied colonies, there is evidence that German politicians identified Britian Muslims, and British Indian Muslims in particular, as a special concern.  The dominant narrative at the time around this proclamation was that Germany heavily influenced and encouraged the Ottomans to do so. The evidence demonstrates that this was an Ottoman decision, and not a German ploy, but that was not the prevailing wisdom at the time.  British newspapers quickly reported on the role of Germany’s Kaiser in this declaration. *The Times* republished a telegram sent from Kaiser Wilhelm to the German Crown Prince on November 19, which read: 
>To his Imperial Highness the German Crown Prince—Sheikh-ul-Islam has published a Fetwa according to which every Musulman must, as a religious duty, fight to the uttermost against England, Russia, and France, the oppressors of Islam. This Fetwa will be distributed throughout the entire Mahomedan world, and is now proclaimed to the pilgrims in Mecca. This means the Holy War for the whole Islamic world. 

The influence of Germany on this declaration is most famously exemplified through the January 1915 publication of Christiaan Snouck Hurgronje’s article, or manifesto, entitled “Heilige Oorlog [Holy War] Made in Germany.” Hurgronje was a Dutch expert on Islam and served as an advisor to the colonial government of the Dutch East Indies. In this article Hurgronje attacked Germany’s cadre of orientalist scholars and accuses them of being the instigators behind the Ottoman jihad proclamation.  His article was immediately translated into English and gained significant traction in academic debates throughout the West, essentially defining the way it was understood by Britons. 
    
In practice, the Ottoman declaration of jihad was not effective as a war strategy; it did not lead to the widespread uprising of Muslims around the world. British commentators at the time had no way to predict the effects of such a declaration and were thus deeply concerned about the effect it would have on their Muslim subjects. At the time of the declaration, the British Empire claimed the largest number of Muslims in the world, with almost half a million Muslims serving in the Indian Army alone.  As one commentator from The Times put it, “A certain amount of anxiety is mingled with indignation…Though a holy war is unlikely, the appearance of Turkey as a belligerent will undoubtedly stir up British Mahomedan subjects.”  
    
There were a variety of reasons why the call to jihad did not result in widespread uprisings from colonized Muslims, such as the fact that many Muslims were simply not prepared to revolt and join the Ottoman Empire. The risks associated with uprising also stopped many from responding to this call. In the British Army, the punishment for sedition was death. As said, there was a particular interest in stirring revolt amongst Muslims in the British army from the Germans and Ottomans. However, Muslims in the British army were unlikely to risk their lives without seeing evidence of the jihad’s success. Due to the lack of any large-scale response to the declaration, the benefits of rebelling did not outweigh the costs (their lives). On top of this, many Muslims did not accept the authority of the Ottoman caliph to speak for the ‘Muslim world.’

Although the Muslims of the British Empire did not take up the call to jihad during the war, British officials were deeply worried, and some of this worry was expressed in British newspapers. Indeed, even though the call to jihad had little effect, the British reaction to it nevertheless revealed their belief that Muslims prioritized their religion above all else and would thus respond to a call to jihad unquestionably. As a result, anti-Muslim language in British newspapers spiked in this period, as it had whenever imperial events involving Muslims occurred since the 1857 Rebellion. Although the level of this anti-Muslim language did not reach the heights that it had in the Rebellion or during Omdurman/Candia, when the lives of British women and children were clearly at stake, the Ottoman call to jihad did results in the emergence of a new word—extremist—as a short-hand for anti-Muslim bigotry in British popular culture, joining older terms like fanatic and fanaticism. After extensive close reading in *The Times*, it appears that the Ottoman declaration of jihad did not intensify existing anti-Muslim rhetoric so much as confirmed it.

##### Methods

My three case studies are all prominent examples of moments of British insecurity, in which Muslims were believed to be the primary opponents. They are each representative of different ways Britons felt their dominance threatened by Muslims during the colonial period. The Rebellion is an example of an uprising of colonial subjects, of which the Rebellion was not the first nor the last example. Omdurman is an example of a battle for colonial conquest, which occurred quite frequently throughout the colonial period, especially at the end of the nineteenth century during the Scramble for Africa. The First World War in general was a moment of global insecurity, felt especially strongly by those with large empires including the British and French. The jihad declaration is an example of the fear that came along with the size of the Empire, specifically the fear of losing control over the colonial subjects who were fighting in their armies. This project considers how this insecurity shaped discourse around Muslims in the empire and how that informed the development of the anti-Muslim rhetoric that exploded once again in the British popular press during the Rushdie Affair.

Although historians agree that these case studies represent moments of increased anti-Muslim rhetoric, no one has yet tried to measure this or demonstrate either change or continuity over time. Digital methods now allow for this. Compared to traditional qualitative research, computational text analysis can identify structures and patterns within the textual corpus on a scale that cannot be discerned by the human eye alone. In addition, word embedding models can also identify words that share similar linguistic contexts, providing a novel angle to the study of historical texts. By using this methodology, this project confidently describes the general patterns and direction of the language around Islam during these case studies, because it considers the entirety of *The Times* for each period. When doing close reading, it is unlikely that a scholar would spend their time reading articles that are seemingly unrelated to the event at hand. My [methodology](https://clnugentmclean.github.io/dissertation/methodology/), because of the decision to include the entirety of the issues of The Times for the whole period, will also be able to pick up any reference to Islam, even if it is a passing comment within an article or an advertisement.
