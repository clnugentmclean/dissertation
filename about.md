This project interrogates the colonial roots of modern British anti-Muslim racism, specifically the idea that Islam is inherently ‘fanatical,’ ‘violent,’ and ‘incompatible’ with the West. The Rushdie Affair in 1989 is often pointed to as the “start” of this discourse by journalists and anthropologists, and the event has been significantly understudied by historians. This dissertation attempts to understand how anti-Muslim hate became normalized in Britain by focusing on some of the stories that were central to its development, namely the Indian Rebellion in 1857, the Battle of Omdurman and Candia Massacre in 1898, and the Ottoman jihad during the First World War. My methodology brings a new perspective to the study of British anti-Muslim thought. I use digital text analysis, specifically word embedding models, on *The Times* of London to chart the construction of British anti-Muslim discourse across these three case studies. This study rests of the foundation that semantic change is a valid proxy for studying broader historical processes. I created corpora of *The Times* around each case study that encompassed between three and four years of discourse and used these as the primary data for analysis. Word embedding models seek to map semantic proximity, which considers the meaning of the words itself within the specific context of the training corpus. I use word vector analysis on corpora of *The Times* of London to chart the consistent British association between fanaticism and Muslims. The continued reference to the so-called fanaticism of Muslims is tied directly to the colonial “civilizing mission” which created hierarchies of savagery amongst colonial populations to justify their subjugation to the British. I argue that while certain turns of phrase are tied to specific time periods, British anti-Muslim rhetoric has deep roots that go back to the colonial period. Specifically, I demonstrate that there is consistency in anti-Muslim rhetoric across the period, but the degree of perceived outrage or danger determined the degree of vitriol and fear in the discourse. I argue that the British consistently portrayed Muslims during the colonial period as fanatics, but the degree to which that is something to be feared is directly correlated with the presence (or not) of British women and children. Intense rhetoric about Muslims seemed to occur during events that damaged the perception of British superiority, and violence against British women certainly hurt this perception. I demonstrate that the rhetoric found in these colonial instances mirrors the modern language used during the Rushdie Affair.
